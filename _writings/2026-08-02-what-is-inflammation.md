Remember the last time you had a cut on your finger? Within seconds, damaged cells released signaling molecules like histamine and cytokines. Nearby blood vessels dilated and became leakier, causing redness, heat, and swelling. White blood cells entered the tissue from the bloodstream to kill bacteria, remove dead tissue, and coordinate repair. Once healing was complete, anti-inflammatory signals shut everything down. This is what a healthy inflammatory response looks like: a clear beginning, a middle, and an end.

Inflammation has earned a bad reputation, but that's only half the story. Without it, even a small cut could become life-threatening. The problem isn't inflammation itself—it's when the response doesn't switch off.

# What is inflammation?

At its core, inflammation is your immune system's repair-and-defense mechanism. Whenever your body detects an infection, injury, or other threat, it dispatches white blood cells, signaling proteins, and increases blood flow to isolate the problem, eliminate the threat, and repair the damaged tissue.

Inflammation is not a disease—it is a biological process. Whether it helps or harms depends largely on its intensity, duration, and context.

Acute inflammation is essential for survival. It is fast, short-lived, and protective, like the swelling after a sprained ankle or a fever during a cold.

However, inflammation can become chronic. Instead of resolving after the threat has passed, the immune system remains mildly activated for months or even years, gradually damaging healthy tissues. Chronic inflammation is low-grade, long-lasting, and often silent, and is associated with numerous chronic diseases.

# Can food trigger inflammation?

Food rarely triggers inflammation in the same way a cut or infection does, unless you have a food allergy, an intolerance, or consume food contaminated with pathogens. Instead, your overall dietary pattern influences many of the biological systems that regulate inflammatory activity over months and years.

Several overlapping mechanisms are thought to contribute.

- **Gut microbiome**

  A healthy gut allows nutrients to pass into the bloodstream while keeping bacteria and bacterial toxins out. Diets low in fiber and high in ultra-processed foods are associated with changes in the gut microbiome that may impair the intestinal barrier in some people. This can allow bacterial fragments, particularly lipopolysaccharides (LPS), to enter the bloodstream, where the immune system recognizes them as foreign and mounts an inflammatory response.

- **Direct metabolic effects**

  Frequent consumption of foods that rapidly raise blood glucose can contribute to oxidative stress over time. During normal metabolism, cells naturally produce Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), which play important roles in cell signaling. However, when ROS production exceeds the body's antioxidant defenses, DNA, proteins, and lipids become damaged, activating inflammatory pathways.

  Advanced Glycation End-products (AGEs) form naturally inside the body when sugars react with proteins and fats. High-temperature cooking methods such as grilling and frying also increase dietary AGE intake. AGEs can activate inflammatory receptors known as RAGE, contributing to chronic inflammatory signaling.

- **Fat composition**

  Our bodies use fatty acids to produce hormone-like signaling molecules called eicosanoids. Omega-3 fatty acids generally promote molecules involved in resolving inflammation, while omega-6 fatty acids are required to produce molecules involved in both initiating and resolving inflammatory responses. Both are essential nutrients, and current evidence does not support avoiding healthy sources of omega-6 fats. However, diets lacking omega-3-rich foods may shift the balance toward a more pro-inflammatory state. Trans fats, on the other hand, are consistently associated with elevated inflammatory markers and should be minimized.

- **Adipose tissue**

  Excess visceral fat is one of the strongest drivers of chronic low-grade inflammation, independent of any specific food. Fat tissue is biologically active. As fat cells enlarge, they release inflammatory signaling molecules and attract immune cells, creating a self-sustaining inflammatory environment that contributes to insulin resistance and metabolic disease.

# How does inflammation affect the body?

Acute inflammation is usually beneficial. It clears pathogens, initiates tissue repair, mobilizes immune cells, and then resolves.

Chronic inflammation, in contrast, occurs when inflammatory signaling persists long after it is needed. Activated immune cells continue producing inflammatory cytokines, ROS, and tissue-remodeling enzymes. Over time, this persistent "friendly fire" damages healthy tissues.

Chronic inflammation is associated with a wide range of diseases:

- **Cardiovascular health:** Damages the endothelial lining of blood vessels, accelerating plaque formation and increasing the risk of heart attack and stroke.

- **Metabolic health:** Inflammatory cytokines interfere with insulin signaling, promoting insulin resistance in muscle and liver cells and increasing the risk of type 2 diabetes.

- **Brain health:** Chronic neuroinflammation is associated with cognitive decline, depression, and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease.

- **Cancer:** Long-standing inflammation contributes to the development of certain cancers by increasing DNA damage, promoting cell proliferation, and altering the surrounding tissue environment.

- **Aging:** Chronic low-grade inflammation ("inflammaging") is now considered one of the biological hallmarks of aging.

# How do I know if I have chronic inflammation?

Chronic inflammation is notoriously difficult to recognize. Unlike a swollen ankle or a sore throat, it often develops gradually and produces vague symptoms that are easy to dismiss.

Possible symptoms include:

- Persistent fatigue
- Joint or muscle aches
- Digestive problems
- Frequent minor infections
- Brain fog
- Poor sleep
- Low mood or increased anxiety

These symptoms are **not specific to chronic inflammation** and can have many other causes. If they persist, it's worth discussing them with your healthcare provider.

Some commonly used clinical markers include:

- **High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP)** is one of the most widely used markers of systemic inflammation. Values below **1 mg/L** are generally considered low cardiovascular risk, **1–3 mg/L** average risk, and **above 3 mg/L** elevated risk. However, infections, injuries, and many medical conditions can temporarily raise hs-CRP, so it should always be interpreted in clinical context.

- **Metabolic health markers.** Fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipid profile, blood pressure, waist circumference, and liver function tests often move alongside chronic inflammation because metabolic dysfunction and inflammation are closely intertwined.

# How to reduce chronic inflammation

The encouraging news is that many of the habits that reduce chronic inflammation are the same habits that improve overall health. There is no single "anti-inflammatory" food or supplement. Instead, the goal is to create an environment where your immune system spends less time in a constant state of activation.

Some of the strongest evidence supports:

- **Eat a mostly whole-food diet**
    - Plenty of vegetables and fruits
    - Legumes
    - Whole grains
    - Nuts and seeds
    - Olive oil
    - Omega-3-rich foods such as fatty fish
    - Fermented foods like yogurt or kefir
    - Limit ultra-processed foods and foods high in trans fats.

    Be cautious of supplements marketed as "anti-inflammatory." Among those with the best evidence are omega-3 fatty acids (particularly if dietary intake is low), vitamin D for people who are deficient, and turmeric (curcumin), although their benefits are generally modest.

- **Exercise regularly**

    Although a strenuous workout temporarily increases inflammatory markers, regular exercise lowers chronic inflammation over time.

    One reason is the **myokine effect**. Contracting skeletal muscles release signaling proteins called myokines, which stimulate anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 while suppressing inflammatory pathways.

    Current guidelines recommend:
    - 150–300 minutes per week of moderate aerobic activity (or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity)
    - Strength training at least twice per week

- **Prioritize sleep**

    Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep each night. Short sleep duration and fragmented sleep are associated with elevated inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP and IL-6.

    Maintaining a consistent sleep schedule also helps regulate hormones such as cortisol and supports healthy immune function.

- **Maintain good oral health**

    Gum disease is a common source of chronic inflammation. Regular brushing, flossing, and dental care can reduce this inflammatory burden.

- **Avoid smoking and limit alcohol**

    Smoking is one of the strongest lifestyle drivers of chronic inflammation. Heavy alcohol consumption also promotes inflammatory signaling and increases the risk of multiple chronic diseases.

Ultimately, there is nothing particularly glamorous about reducing chronic inflammation. Eat well. Exercise regularly. Sleep enough. Maintain a healthy weight. Take care of your teeth. Don't smoke.

These same habits consistently reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, certain cancers, and premature death. The lifestyle that supports a healthy inflammatory response is, unsurprisingly, the same lifestyle that supports a longer and healthier life.
